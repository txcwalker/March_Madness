- `src/march_madness/analysis/round_advancement.py` — `round_advancement_counts()`, `average_wins_by_team()` (see Fragile Areas — has a non-obvious correct denominator), `wins_over_seed_expectation()` (compares to `HISTORICAL_AVERAGE_WINS_BY_SEED`, a cited real external baseline), `cinderella_probability()`, `final_four_combination_counts()`, `benefit_if_team_loses()` (Milestone 5 -- "loses" means not reaching the Final Four/R4; computed purely from `simulation_results.csv`, no new simulation; returns BOTH championship-odds and Final-Four-odds shares/benefit per (TeamX, TeamY) pair, each as TeamY's **baseline (unconditional, full-sample) odds** vs. TeamY's odds conditioned on TeamX being eliminated -- see Fragile Areas for why the baseline is the unconditional rate and not TeamY's odds conditioned on TeamX *advancing* (an earlier version, and a real bug the user caught). **Final Four odds are region-scoped, Championship odds are not** (2026-07-31 correction -- see Fragile Areas): for a TeamY outside TeamX's own region, the conditioned *Final Four* column is set equal to TeamY's baseline, forcing FinalFourBenefit to exactly 0 -- Final Four odds are mathematically guaranteed unaffected by a different-region TeamX, so any nonzero movement there would be pure Monte Carlo sampling noise. Championship odds do NOT get this treatment for any TeamY, same-region or not -- eliminating a strong TeamX genuinely makes the championship game easier for everyone else, not just TeamX's own region, so `ChampionShareIfXEliminated` always uses the real TeamX-eliminated bracket subset. Don't apply the Final Four region-forcing logic to championship odds, and don't remove it from Final Four odds -- they're correct as different treatments, not an inconsistency to "fix"). All generalized from the legacy project's per-season-hardcoded versions — none of these take a team name or region dict as a hardcoded constant.
- `src/march_madness/analysis/bracket_path.py` — Milestone 5's Path of Least Resistance, on its third design (2026-07-31; see Fragile Areas for why the first two were superseded). `build_feeds_into(slots)` maps every seed/slot code to the slot it feeds into plus its opponent code; `average_win_probability(win_probabilities)` is a simple, self-contained team-strength score (mean win probability against the whole field); `resolve_slot_winners_by_bracket(results)` groups `bracket.simulate.run_monte_carlo()`'s long-format output into one `{slot: winner}` lookup per simulated bracket. `path_of_least_resistance(slots, seed_to_team, win_probabilities, results)` walks each team's own seed forward through R1-R4 across all 10,000 already-run simulated brackets, and at each hop looks up whichever team **actually won** the opposing slot in that specific bracket (not a single hypothetical best- or worst-case team). Produces two metrics per team: **PathStrengthFaced** (average of the *opponents'* own strength scores, never the team's own win probability — deliberately seed-agnostic, so a 1-seed's inherent strength can't make its draw look artificially easy; the headline "how tough is the draw" number, sorted ascending = easiest first) and **PathEase** (average, across brackets, of the team's own win-probability product against the opponents it actually drew — kept as the secondary "given that draw, are you still favored" number; product not sum within a bracket, since sequential elimination games compound).
- `src/march_madness/analysis/region_strength.py` — `region_of_seed()` derives region from the seed code's first letter (no 56-team hardcoded dict like the legacy project needed); `region_championship_counts()`; `region_top_seed_final_four_share()` (the **primary** region-fragility metric as of 2026-07-30 — unconditional across all simulated brackets); `region_top_seed_championship_share()` (the older, conditioned-on-winning-the-title version — kept as a secondary stat, not deleted, but do not treat it as the headline number; see its docstring for why it's both conceptually conflated and statistically unstable). `region_of_main_bracket_slot()` (2026-08-03) pulls the region letter out of an R1-R4 slot code (e.g. `"R2W1"` -> `"W"`; `None` for a play-in slot or the cross-region R5/R6 slots). `region_competitiveness()` (needs `win_probabilities` + the raw `results`, imports `resolve_slot_winners_by_bracket()` from `bracket_path.py`) averages **outcome uncertainty** — `2 × (1 - favorite's win probability)` — across every game actually played within a region, all 10,000 brackets; see Fragile Areas for why this is explicitly NOT a margin-of-victory metric. `strongest_team_by_region()` picks each region's model-implied favorite via `average_win_probability()` (imported from `bracket_path.py`), deliberately not the #1 seed — same reasoning as the retired Seed Prediction milestone above. `region_effective_contenders()` is 1/HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) on `round_advancement.benefit_if_team_loses()`'s same-region `FinalFourShareIfXEliminated` values — "how many realistic backups does this region have if its favorite falls," reusing `benefit_if_team_loses()`'s output rather than a new simulation. These three are computed in `run_pipeline.py` (win-probabilities needed) and written to `region_competitiveness.csv`; `export_site_data.py` reads that CSV and computes `region_effective_contenders()` itself from the `benefit` DataFrame it already builds for the Who Benefits payload (shared, not recomputed).
- `scripts/run_pipeline.py` — the entry point: `python scripts/run_pipeline.py [--model {logistic_regression,random_forest,xgboost_model,neural_net}] [--n-brackets N] [--config PATH]` (default model: `xgboost_model`, see Current Priorities). ## Rules & Coding Style

- No import-time side effects. Modules define functions/classes; execution happens via `scripts/` entry points or `if __name__ == "__main__"`.
- Notebooks (`notebooks/`) are exploration-only. Nothing in `src/` imports from a notebook, and notebook logic is never hand-copied into `src/` without being rewritten as tested functions.
- Season year, bracket size, round count, and play-in count come from `config/season.yaml` — never hardcode a year or team count in `src/`.
- One canonical implementation per model — no duplicate `.py`/`.ipynb` versions of the same logic.
- Comment aggressively per global standards: docstrings state Inputs, Outputs, Purpose, and any non-obvious logic (e.g., KenPom pagination quirks).

## Fragile Areas

- **KenPom ingest**: raw export is manually copy/pasted (KenPom is subscription-gated and blocks scraping — do not attempt to automate the fetch itself). The paginated source table repeats its header row every ~40 teams and prints a rank subscript next to every stat column. `ingest/kenpom.py` must strip both before merging into history. Get this wrong and every downstream model silently trains on garbage rows.
- **Bracket structure**: `R1`..`R6` and the 64-team main bracket are a *fixed* convention from Kaggle's own data (`MNCAATourneySlots.csv`) — coding them as constants in `bracket/structure.py` is correct, not something to abstract away. What's config-driven is `num_play_in_games`; the invariant `bracket.size == 64 + num_play_in_games` is enforced by `validate_bracket_config()`. A 76-team format is `num_play_in_games=12`, not a different round structure.
- **Play-in slot ordering in raw Kaggle data**: in `MNCAATourneySlots.csv`, play-in slots (e.g. `X16`, defined by `X16a`/`X16b`) are listed *after* the R1 rows that reference them as a seed — confirmed against the real 2024 file. The legacy simulator (`sims_mens.py`) iterated rows in raw file order with no sort, so it would look up an unresolved play-in winner for any R1 slot involving a play-in team. Always run slot data through `order_slots_for_simulation()` before simulating — never assume raw file order is resolution order.
- **Excel date-mangles KenPom's W-L column, in BOTH directions**: pasting KenPom's raw table into Excel silently reformats a `W-L` value into a date whenever *either* side looks like a month number (1-12) -- `20-12` (losses=12) becomes `20-Dec`, confirmed in `kenpom_2026_raw.csv` (~90 of 365 teams, 25%); but `2-29` (wins=2) becomes `Feb-29`, confirmed in the backfilled 2010/2012 historical data (Alcorn St., Binghamton) -- a mirror-image case the original fix didn't cover since the 2026 raw export never happened to trigger it. `parse_win_loss_component()` (was `parse_losses` -- renamed when this was found, since "losses" was no longer accurate) recovers the month on *either* side; don't "simplify" that away or revert to applying it to only one side of the split -- it's fixing real, silent data corruption (an unrecovered case produces `NaN`, which doesn't fail loudly until it crashes model training several layers deep in sklearn).
- **KenPom team names are not Kaggle `TeamID`s**: `ingest/kenpom.py` deliberately does not reconcile KenPom's team-name spellings against Kaggle's `TeamID`s — that reconciliation belongs in `features/build_features.py`, where KenPom and Kaggle data actually get joined. Don't add name-matching logic to the ingest layer.
- **Exact-name matching silently drops over a third of teams**: verified on the real 2026 data — matching KenPom's `Team` string exactly against Kaggle's `TeamName` (what the legacy project did) matched only 228/365 teams (62%); KenPom's "Iowa St." never equals Kaggle's "Iowa State". `match_kenpom_teams()` matches against `MTeamSpellings.csv` instead (352/365, 96%) and returns the remainder as `unmatched` so gaps stay visible. Never go back to a plain `TeamName` merge.
- **KenPom's conference abbreviations drift over time**, the same way `AdjEM` became `NetRtg` (see the KenPom ingest note above). The legacy `conf_mapping` used `Pat` for the Patriot League; the real 2026 export uses `PL`. Verified: without `BSth`/`BW`/`PL`/`SC` in `KENPOM_TO_KAGGLE_CONFERENCE`, 10% of matchup rows got a silently null conference. If a future season's `Conf_A`/`Conf_B` shows up null unexpectedly, check for a new/renamed KenPom conference abbreviation here first.
- **Positional alignment in a two-sided join is a real bug, not a hypothetical**: the legacy project's tournament-game merge joined winner-rows to loser-rows by row position (`left_index=True, right_index=True`) rather than a real key. If either side's join with KenPom stats drops a different set of rows, this silently pairs unrelated games together. `build_matchup_history()` joins on an explicit `GameID` instead — any change to that join must preserve the explicit key, never revert to positional/index alignment.
- **Training-data volume was the real cause of a large model-accuracy gap vs. the legacy project, not model choice**: as of 2026-07-30, `data/raw/`+`data/processed/` together now have 24 seasons (2003-2026, ~116K games) via `scripts/backfill_historical_kenpom.py` — before that backfill, only 2026 (5,265 games) was populated, a ~23x reduction vs. the legacy project's 23-season training set. That data starvation hit high-variance learners (xgboost_model, neural_net) far harder than logistic_regression/random_forest (see `scripts/evaluate_models.py`, WORKLOG 2026-07-30 for real before/after numbers). `seed_knn` specifically benefits too — `best_k`/accuracy jumped substantially post-backfill (also see WORKLOG) — but its `mean_absolute_seed_error` got *worse* post-backfill, an oddity not yet investigated; don't assume seed_knn is "fixed," just less starved.
- **`compute_win_probabilities()` symmetrizes for a real reason -- don't simplify it back to a single raw `predict_proba` call**: a real audit (2026-07-30, prompted by Duke's simulated title share looking implausible -- 67.5% under xgboost_model despite Arizona/Michigan having nearly identical KenPom ratings) found the model is NOT slot-invariant: the same real matchup gave meaningfully different probabilities depending on which team's stats landed in the "_A" feature slot vs "_B" (confirmed on real 2026 data -- Arizona vs Michigan came back 64%/38% one way, ~38%/62% the other, for teams that should be close to a coin flip). `simulate_bracket` always resolves a slot's StrongSeed as "_A", so an un-symmetrized bias gives whichever team's bracket position is labeled StrongSeed a compounding, unearned edge every round. Fixed by averaging the forward prediction against the complementary probability implied by the reverse-slot prediction — `probs[a][b] + probs[b][a] == 1.0` always holds now. Confirmed via `run_pipeline.py`: Duke's title share dropped from 67.5% to 41.0% with nothing else changed. `randomize_matchup_sides()` (training-time) does NOT make this unnecessary — it only ensures the model doesn't learn a systematic "A tends to win" pattern from historical winner/loser ordering; it does nothing to guarantee the fitted model is exactly symmetric for any *specific* matchup, especially for high-capacity models like XGBoost. `logistic_regression` looked close to slot-invariant in this audit (a genuinely linear model, StandardScaler included, has less room to learn this artifact) but was not exhaustively verified — don't assume any given model is safe without checking.
- **Legacy's `TeamID_A`/`TeamID_B` features were a real identity-leakage bug, not a design choice to reconsider casually**: the legacy project's row-random (not season-aware) train/test split let the same team's raw ID appear on both sides, letting tree/NN models partially memorize a team's win rate via its ID rather than learn from stats — inflating legacy's old RF/XGBoost/NN numbers. `models/common.py` correctly excludes `TeamID_A/B`. The user has discussed real, non-leaky reasons a program-identity feature might be legitimate signal in the NIL/transfer-portal era and is open to testing one properly at some point — but that's a season-aware, non-leaky feature to design deliberately, not restoring the raw ID columns. (Previously filed under Milestone 3, which retired 2026-08-03 for unrelated reasons; this idea is unaffected, just unscheduled.)
- **`XGBClassifier(use_label_encoder=...)` is gone**: the legacy project passed `use_label_encoder=False`, a parameter modern `xgboost` removed entirely — passing it now raises a `TypeError`. `models/xgboost_model.py` doesn't pass it. If you see that parameter reappear anywhere (e.g. copied from an old snippet), delete it.
- **No plotting in `models/`**: the legacy model scripts each had ~150-250 lines of near-duplicated ROC/calibration/confusion-matrix plotting inline. That's a Milestone 2 (presentation) concern, deliberately left out of `models/common.py`'s `evaluate_classifier()` — it returns a metrics dict, not plots. Don't add matplotlib calls back into `models/`.
- **A given season's `MNCAATourneySeeds.csv`/`MNCAATourneySlots.csv` may or may not include play-in detail**: confirmed by comparing real seasons — 2024's slots file has 8 play-in seed entries (a/b codes) and 67 total slot rows (4 play-in + 63 main bracket); the 2026 snapshot in this repo has *zero* play-in rows and exactly 64 seeds / 63 slots, i.e. First Four already resolved into a clean 64-team field. Don't assume every season's raw data has the same `num_play_in_games` as `config/season.yaml` — always run real season data through `bracket.structure.validate_slots()` with a `BracketConfig` matching what's *actually in that file*, not blindly reused from the global config.
- **Team-name match gaps are individually identifiable, not just a percentage**: e.g. "Queens NC" (TeamID 1474, a real 2026 tournament team, seed Z15) has no `MTeamSpellings.csv` entry and is part of the ~4% `match_kenpom_teams()` doesn't resolve. A real pipeline run needs an explicit strategy for teams like this (exclude, impute average stats, or extend the spellings lookup) — silently crashing on a `KeyError` mid-simulation is the failure mode to avoid.
- **`average_wins_by_team()`'s denominator is not "brackets this team appears in"**: `bracket.simulate`'s results only record the *winner* of each slot, so a team that loses its very first game in every single bracket never appears in `results` at all. Averaging only over the brackets a team does appear in (the naively "obvious" implementation, and a real bug this caught before shipping) silently inflates weak teams' averages by excluding their 0-win brackets from the denominator entirely. The fix requires the full `team_ids` field passed in explicitly and dividing total wins by the *total* bracket count — `results` alone can't tell you a team existed if it never won anything.
- **Splitting 10,000 brackets into two conditioned subsets can show a nonzero "effect" that's pure sampling noise, not signal**: found via `benefit_if_team_loses()` (see Active Files) -- an early version conditioned every TeamY on TeamX's Final Four fate with no region check, and different-region teams showed small nonzero movement (e.g. +0.6 percentage points) that the user correctly flagged as impossible (regions resolve independently; a different-region team's Final Four odds are mathematically guaranteed unaffected by TeamX). Verified the magnitude against the expected standard error for that split size (`sqrt(p(1-p)(1/n1+1/n2))`) — it matched almost exactly, confirming noise, not a code bug in the conditioning formula itself. General lesson for any future conditional-probability analysis on the Monte Carlo results: before trusting a nonzero conditioned difference, check whether the two things being conditioned on are actually causally/structurally linked (here: same region) — if not, any observed movement is sampling noise and should be zeroed out using domain knowledge, not reported as a real effect. A 10,000-bracket sample is not infinite; don't assume every empirical conditional difference reflects a real dependency.
- **`benefit_if_team_loses()`'s "before" value must be TeamY's unconditional baseline, not TeamY's odds conditioned on TeamX *advancing***: an earlier version used the "TeamX reaches the Final Four" subset as the before-value, which the user caught by testing a heavy underdog (Akron, effectively never reaches the Final Four in any simulated bracket) — every TeamY's "before" collapsed to ~0% regardless of TeamY's real odds, since that conditioning subset was tiny or empty, making the frontend read as "TeamY has basically no chance today" (false) rather than "TeamY's normal odds are X%." Fixed by using the full-sample unconditional rate as baseline for every TeamY, same-region or not (the same quantity already computed as `overall_champion_share`/`overall_final_four_share` for the cross-region case, now reused everywhere). A useful side effect: for a TeamX this lopsided, baseline and the TeamX-eliminated conditioned value now come out nearly identical for everyone (correctly showing "eliminating a team that was already a near-lock loser doesn't meaningfully help anyone") — don't mistake that near-zero delta for a bug; verify with a real, competitive TeamX (e.g. a 4-8 seed, or any team with a real Final Four share) if checking this metric produces a visible before/after gap.
- **Final Four odds and Championship odds need different region-scoping in `benefit_if_team_loses()` -- this is correct, not an inconsistency**: an earlier version (2026-07-30) forced BOTH metrics to zero out for a different-region TeamY, reasoning that any cross-region effect would be indistinguishable from Monte Carlo sampling noise. The user correctly pushed back (2026-07-31) on this for championship odds specifically: verified against real 2026 data with Duke as TeamX -- Michigan and Arizona (both outside Duke's region; their own Final Four odds correctly stay exactly flat, 68.2%/58.2%, confirming they're cross-region) still show a real, substantial championship-odds gain if Duke is eliminated (17.2%→29.6% and 21.1%→29.6%). This makes sense once you separate the two questions: "does TeamY *reach* the championship game" is unaffected by a different-region TeamX (proven -- see the Final Four note above), but "does TeamY *win* the championship game" depends on who they'd face there, which comes from the other half of the bracket including TeamX's region -- eliminating a strong TeamX gives literally every other team, regardless of region, an easier eventual final. Only Final Four odds get the region-forcing treatment now; championship odds always use the real TeamX-eliminated subset for every TeamY.
- **Women's tournament**: KenPom has no women's coverage. Do not assume the men's ingest pipeline generalizes — a different ratings source (candidate: Massey Ordinals, already in the Kaggle bundle) will be needed when this is tackled.
- **`region_competitiveness()`'s "outcome uncertainty" is NOT a margin-of-victory metric — don't let it drift into meaning that**: the user flagged this directly during design (2026-08-03) — a 90/10 favorite winning by one point and a 55/45 game being a blowout are both completely possible; this metric only ever looks at pregame win probability, never a simulated or real score. It answers "how unpredictable is the *winner*," full stop. If a future revision wants an actual margin-of-victory or "nail-biter" stat, that needs real per-game score data (not modeled here) and should be a clearly-separate metric, not a reinterpretation of this one.
- **Path of Least Resistance's raw PathEase mechanically favors strong teams, independent of draw difficulty — don't treat it as a draw-difficulty metric on its own**: PathEase is the product of a team's *own* win probability against its opponents, and a 1-seed's win probability is high against almost anyone regardless of who they actually drew — so ranking by PathEase alone put strong teams at the top even when the underlying user question was "how tough is the draw," not "how good is the team." Confirmed by the metric's own history: v1 (single most-likely opponent per round) and v2 (2026-07-30, single toughest-possible opponent per round via `build_subtree_teams()`) both still ranked almost entirely by team strength, because both fed the chosen opponent(s) through the team's own win probability with no normalization. v3 (2026-07-31) fixes this by splitting into two metrics that never get collapsed into one number: `PathStrengthFaced` (opponents' own strength only, never the team's own win probability — this is what should be used to answer "how tough is the draw") alongside `PathEase` (kept as the separate, team-relative "am I still favored" number). If a future revision is tempted to rank by PathEase again "for simplicity," it will reintroduce the same seed-correlation problem this fixed.
